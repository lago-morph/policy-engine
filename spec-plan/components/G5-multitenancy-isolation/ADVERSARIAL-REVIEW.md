# G5 — Multi-Tenancy Isolation — ADVERSARIAL REVIEW

**Red-team persona:** (a) a malicious tenant trying to read/corrupt another tenant's *audit log* (the catastrophic prize for a compliance product); (b) a regulated buyer's security architect deciding whether to sign; (c) a pen-tester attacking the seams between G5 and D1/D2/C2/C3/F2/G1/G4/G7.
**Target:** `SPEC.md`, `PLAN.md`, `ALT-tenancy-models.md`.
**Stance:** G5 *names* the right problem (the board's "WHERE clause = tenancy" finding) and proposes a dial. This review attacks whether the dial actually closes the holes or just *relocates* them, and whether the regulated buyer is real-world satisfiable.

---

## 1. Attacks on the core thesis (the WHERE-clause / shared-substrate problem)

**G5-A1 — T1 "RLS makes a forgotten predicate safe" is half-true; the object-store log has no RLS, and that's where the crown jewels live.** The SPEC's strongest T1 claim is "a forgotten interceptor still fails closed at the RLS engine" (G5-R1). But the **system of record is C2's append-only object-store log** (meta-adversarial G-3), and **object stores have no RLS** (the SPEC concedes this in G5-R2/OQ-G5-4). So the WHERE-clause-only failure the board warns about is *fully* mitigated only for relational read-models, and the relational store is the *derived* data — the **raw evidence** (the thing a cross-tenant leak makes catastrophic) is protected at T1 by a "per-tenant key prefix + per-request scoped credential," which is **exactly an application-layer guard with exactly the bypass the board condemned** — a credential-minting choke point one bug away from minting a too-broad credential. **Net: G5 has *moved* the single-choke-point risk from "the predicate" to "the per-request credential minter," not eliminated it, for the most sensitive data at T1.** *Severity: CRITICAL.* The honest conclusion the SPEC half-states but should state loudly: **T1 cannot protect the raw evidence log to a regulated standard — which is the real reason regulated buyers must be on T2 (per-bucket), and T1 must be sold only where a *raw-evidence cross-tenant leak is tolerable*.** The SPEC's D-G5-1 ("regulated ⇒ T2 minimum") is therefore not a sales nicety; it's a *correctness requirement*, and the SPEC should frame it as such rather than as positioning.

**G5-A2 — Crypto-shredding as the "primary deletion guarantee" (G5-R25) has a fatal interaction with the per-tenant chain and with backups.** Deletion-by-key-destruction assumes (i) the tenant key encrypts *all* of that tenant's at-rest evidence and (ii) no plaintext copy escaped. But: (a) **analytics materialized views** (W4) and **the relational read-model** (W1) are *derived* from the evidence — are *they* encrypted under the same per-tenant key? If a C3 aggregate or a C5 report cached a tenant's data in a differently-keyed store, crypto-shred leaves a readable residue. (b) **Backups (G3)** taken before key destruction still contain the (key-wrapped) data, and if backup encryption uses a *separate* key, the tenant data survives crypto-shred in backups. (c) **The per-tenant key itself is held by G4/KMS, which is backed up.** Destroying "the key" must mean destroying every copy *including KMS backups and key escrow* — a far stronger claim than "delete the key." **Severity: CRITICAL** for a product that issues a *signed deletion certificate* (G5-R24): if the certificate asserts erasure that crypto-shred didn't actually achieve (because of a derived copy or a backup), the platform has committed the exact "declared vs verified" sin (XD-6) at the deletion layer — and a false deletion certificate is a *regulatory liability*, worse than no certificate. The SPEC must specify: every derived store keyed under the same tenant key, a backup-retention/erasure interaction with G3, and a KMS key-destruction that covers escrow/backups — and the certificate must scope its claim to what was *actually* destroyed (e.g. "primary + read-model erased; backups expire by <date>").

**G5-A3 — The per-tenant hash chain (OQ-G5-3) breaks cross-tenant tamper-evidence and the platform-wide integrity story.** G5 mandates a *per-tenant* chain so deletion has no collateral (G5-R26). Correct for deletion — but it **fragments the integrity guarantee**: there is now no single chain proving "no evidence was deleted *platform-wide*," only N independent chains. A malicious *platform operator* (insider) could drop an entire tenant's chain (delete the tenant) and no surviving chain would notice — whereas a global chain would show a gap. So G5 trades *tenant-deletion-safety* for *loss of platform-level completeness evidence*. For a compliance product this is a real tension, not a free win. **Severity: HIGH.** Mitigation the SPEC omits: a thin **per-tenant chain-head registry** (the registry, §5, records each tenant's current chain head, itself in a global, append-only, signed meta-log) so that *tenant existence + chain-head* is globally tamper-evident even though *content* chains are per-tenant. Without this, "delete the whole tenant quietly" is undetectable.

## 2. Attacks on the identity / claim-integrity plane (tenancy rests on the claim)

**G5-A4 — Registry-binding the claim (G5-R13/R14) moves trust to the registry, which is edited by an admin whose own boundary is... the same platform.** G5's answer to "tenancy rests on an attacker-influenced claim" is "validate the claim against the server-side registry + issuer binding." Good — but it relocates the trust root to **the registry edit operation**, which is a Platform Governance Admin action (G5-R17). D2-A6 already found admin verbs are *preventively uncontrolled* (audit-only). G5 says registry edits are dual-controlled (good, it adopts the fix) — but **the issuer→tenant binding (G5-R14) is the new keystone**: whoever can add a `trusted_issuer` to a tenant can mint that tenant's identity. A compromised/colluding admin adds their own realm as a trusted issuer for `payments`, then asserts `tenant=payments` legitimately. The dual-control mitigates collusion-of-one, not collusion-of-two, and **there is no detection** that an issuer binding *changed* feeding into the security-operator's HL-13 view. **Severity: HIGH.** The SPEC should make issuer-binding changes a *first-class, alertable security event* (not just an audit row) and SHOULD require the *tenant's own* sign-off (not just two platform admins) to add an issuer to that tenant — otherwise the platform operator unilaterally controls every tenant's identity root.

**G5-A5 — "Unknown tenant ⇒ degraded/fail closed" (G5-R13) collides with onboarding and with multi-tenant-by-the-same-human.** A brand-new tenant, or a human legitimately belonging to two tenants (a consultant, an MSP operator), produces claims the registry may not yet fully bind. Fail-closed is right for security but **operationally brittle**: every onboarding has a window where the tenant exists in the registry but issuer binding/scope defaults aren't propagated, and a human-in-two-tenants hits the issuer-binding check (their token from realm A asserting work in tenant B). The SPEC's `provisioning` state (G5-R21) helps for onboarding but says nothing about **legitimately multi-tenant principals** (MSPs managing many tenants are a real regulated buyer pattern). *Severity: MEDIUM.* Needs an explicit model for a principal scoped to multiple tenants under T2+ physical separation (does the MSP admin get N credentials, one per tenant? how does the console switch tenant context without crossing a boundary?). Unspecified.

## 3. Attacks on the noisy-neighbor / compute plane

**G5-A6 — The serial E1 replay core (G5-R9) is a per-tenant *fairness* control, but fairness ≠ isolation; a side-channel and a DoS survive.** G5 puts a per-tenant fair queue over the serial replay core. This bounds *throughput* per tenant. It does **not** prevent: (a) a **timing side-channel** — tenant A measures its own replay latency and infers tenant B's load/activity (when B runs large jobs), leaking B's *operational metadata* (e.g. "B ran a huge compliance sweep on the 14th" — itself sensitive in M&A/incident contexts); (b) a **convoy DoS** — even fair-queued, a serial core means a tenant submitting many *small* jobs still inflates everyone's queue depth within their fair share's scheduling. For T1/T2 (shared serial core) this is an accepted residual, but the SPEC files it under "disclosed side-channel" without quantifying it. **Severity: MEDIUM** (HIGH for a tenant whose *activity timing* is itself confidential — common in regulated finance). The honest fix is that **timing-sensitive tenants need T3 (dedicated compute)**, which the SPEC implies but doesn't state as a *driver* for choosing T3 (it frames T3 as data-sovereignty-driven; it's also *side-channel*-driven).

**G5-A7 — Per-tenant quota requires accurate per-tenant attribution, which the shared planes can't always give.** G5-R8 caps each tenant's share of shared workers. But ingest/analytics work is sometimes **cross-tenant by nature** (the global HL-13 view; a shared catalog refresh; the per-tenant-chain-head meta-log from G5-A3). Whose quota does a *global* analytics job count against? If it's unattributed, a global-view abuser (a malicious security-operator) bypasses all per-tenant quotas by routing work through the global path. *Severity: MEDIUM.* The global path (G5-R5) needs its *own* quota and its own abuse model, which the SPEC omits.

## 4. Attacks on residency

**G5-A8 — Residency is enforced "at write/replicate/restore" but the control plane and the *registry itself* are global.** G5-R11 keeps tenant *evidence* in-region but lets the control plane hold *metadata* globally. For a strict data-sovereignty regime (some EU/financial regulators), **even metadata** (that tenant X exists, its tenant_id, its volume, its activity timing) may be regulated — and the **registry** (§5), which holds residency + issuer bindings + quotas for *all* tenants, is a single global object. A subpoena/seizure of the global control plane exposes the *existence and shape* of every tenant including the data-sovereign ones. *Severity: MEDIUM-HIGH for true sovereignty buyers.* The SPEC's T4 ("optionally remove metadata for data-sovereign tenants," ALT §4.3) hand-waves this; it needs a real model for a **regionally-partitioned registry** (or a sovereign tenant that the global plane genuinely cannot see), or it must tell sovereign buyers honestly that the *fact of their existence* lives in the global plane.

**G5-A9 — Residency + the global analytics view are in direct contradiction, and the SPEC resolves it by *excluding* the residency-restricted tenant (G5-R12) — which silently makes the "platform-wide attack pattern" view a lie.** HL-13 wants "show platform-wide attack patterns." G5-R12 excludes `allowCrossRegionAggregation=false` tenants from that view. So the security operator's "platform-wide" view is **actually "platform-wide except the tenants who opted out"** — and if those are the high-value regulated tenants, the cross-tenant-attack-detection feature (a selling point) is blindest exactly where attacks matter most, **without telling the operator the view is partial.** *Severity: MEDIUM.* Mirrors the corpus's own XD-6 "declared vs verified" sin: a "platform-wide" label on a partial view. Fix: the global view must surface its *coverage denominator* ("N of M tenants included; K excluded for residency") — exactly the honesty discipline the rest of the platform mandates and this view skips.

## 5. Attacks on lifecycle & migration

**G5-A10 — T1→T2 migration's "copy-with-verify then cutover" has a dual-write/consistency window the SPEC declares away.** PLAN §4.1 says cutover flips `TenantStore` resolution "with no window of cross-boundary readability." But during the copy (step 3), the tenant is *live at T1* (still writing new evidence to the shared store) while data is being copied to T2. New writes during the copy either (a) must be dual-written to both (a consistency/ordering hazard on an append-only chain — the chain head moves under you), or (b) require a write freeze (a downtime the SPEC doesn't admit). Re-chaining a *moving* per-tenant chain mid-copy is a real correctness hazard (which event is the new head?). *Severity: HIGH* — this is the classic live-migration-of-an-append-only-log problem and the SPEC waves it through. Needs either a **write-freeze window** (state it honestly) or a **chain-fork-and-reconcile** protocol with a defined linearization point.

**G5-A11 — Suspend "preserves read + chain" but a suspended tenant still consumes storage/quota and still has live keys — suspension isn't a security state, it's a billing state.** G5-R22 freezes writes but keeps everything readable. A *compromised* tenant (the reason you'd suspend in an incident) still has live credentials, live keys, and read access during suspension. Suspend-for-incident and suspend-for-nonpayment are conflated. *Severity: MEDIUM.* Need a **`quarantine`** state distinct from `suspend`: revoke credentials/keys-access, freeze reads too, preserve the chain for forensics. The SPEC's single suspend state can't serve incident response.

**G5-A12 — The deletion certificate (G5-R24) is signed by the platform key — the same trust root the deleted tenant no longer trusts.** A tenant offboarding (possibly *because* of a dispute) receives a deletion certificate signed by the platform's own key. For it to be evidence in a dispute *with the platform*, it can't rest solely on the platform's signature. *Severity: LOW-MEDIUM.* Consider a transparency-log anchor / third-party timestamp so the certificate is verifiable independent of continued platform good-faith.

## 6. Cross-component & scope-creep findings

**G5-A13 — G5 imposes a *breaking* C2 contract change (per-tenant chain, OQ-G5-3) and the corpus's own meta-adversarial finding is that C2 changes are an unplanned, unmodeled migration (Risk #8).** G5 cheerfully requires C2 to re-architect its hash chain from global to per-tenant. That's the *right* design, but it lands on C2 as exactly the breaking-change-with-no-migration-plan the meta-board flagged. **G5 must own a slice of the C2 migration**, not just declare the requirement. *Severity: HIGH (coordination).* Flag to cross-cutting reconciliation as a hard dependency with a shared migration plan — the PLAN.md flags it but understates that it *is* a C2 re-architecture.

**G5-A14 — G5 quietly expands D2, D1, C2, C3, F2, G1, G3, G4, G7 — it is a cross-cutting *contract* dressed as a component.** Nearly every G5 requirement is actually a mandate on another component (RLS-mandatory on D2, issuer-binding on D1, per-tenant chain on C2, scoped analytics on C3/C5, region-spokes on F2, per-tenant quota on G1, per-tenant keys on G4, erasure on G7). G5 owns almost no code of its own except the registry + abstractions. **Risk:** as a "component," G5's requirements get filed as *its* backlog and never land in the owning components' plans (the exact meta-adversarial M-1 failure: the fix sits in one doc, the owning docs don't change). *Severity: HIGH (process).* G5 should be explicit that it is a **tenancy *contract* across components** (like INTER-DOMAIN-CONTRACTS), and each imposed requirement must be *propagated into the owning component's SPEC*, or it won't be built.

**G5-A15 — "Per-tenant dial buildable as one product" rests on three abstractions that don't exist yet and that every other component must adopt.** The `TenantStore`/`TenantCompute`/`TenantKey` indirections (ALT §5.5) are the load-bearing claim. But D2, C2, C3, E1, F2 were all spec'd *without* them — they assume a single store/pool/key. Retrofitting every storage/compute/key access in the corpus to go through a tenant-resolving abstraction is a large, invasive change the PLAN treats as "W1/W2 build the abstraction" but doesn't account for *every consumer adopting it*. If even one component keeps a direct store/key handle (the D2-A1 lint problem, one layer up), the dial leaks. *Severity: HIGH.* The abstraction must be enforced the way D2-R3 tries to (no direct handle reachable) — and that enforcement is the same lint-vs-control weakness, now for tenancy.

## 8. Verdict

G5 correctly identifies and *names* the catastrophic finding the rest of the corpus left unowned — "tenancy = a WHERE clause over a shared evidence log" — and the **per-tenant dial + three abstractions** is the right shape: it makes "regulated needs hard isolation" and "don't build four products" and "migrate in place" simultaneously true. The ALT's pooled/bridge/silo analysis is sound and the T2-as-keystone call is right.

**But three things will hurt in production, in order:**

1. **Deletion (G5-A2) is the sharpest edge.** Crypto-shred + a *signed deletion certificate* is a bold promise that, if the derived-copy/backup/KMS-escrow residue isn't handled, is a *false* compliance attestation — the platform's own cardinal sin, at the deletion layer. This must be airtight before any certificate is issued.

2. **T1 is weaker than the SPEC's framing admits (G5-A1):** the raw evidence log, the actual prize, falls back to an app-layer credential minter at T1 — the exact choke-point class the board condemned. The SPEC half-says this; it must say it loudly and make "regulated ⇒ T2" a correctness rule, not sales guidance.

3. **G5 is a contract, not a component (G5-A14/A13/A15):** almost every requirement is a mandate on another component, riding on three abstractions nobody else has adopted yet. The single largest risk is the meta-adversarial M-1 failure repeating: the tenancy fixes live in *this* document and never propagate into D1/D2/C2/C3/F2/G1/G4/G7. **G5 succeeds only if Wave-3 reconciliation pushes each mandate into the owning SPEC and enforces the abstractions architecturally.**

The architecture is the right one. The danger is that, like the WHERE clause it replaces, the *enforcement* of G5's own boundaries ends up resting on lints, abstractions-by-convention, and a deletion certificate that promises more than crypto-shred delivers.
