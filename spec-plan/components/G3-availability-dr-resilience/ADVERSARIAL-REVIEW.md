# G3 — Availability, DR & Resilience — ADVERSARIAL REVIEW

**Red-team persona.** Date: 2026-05-30. Mandate: attack the G3 SPEC's assumptions, find where it lies to itself, and rank the defects. The three attacks the brief named explicitly — **split-brain on the hash chain after a partial restore (A-1)**, **the circuit breaker becoming a bypass (A-4)**, and **RPO>0 losing audit events, fatal to the compliance thesis (A-3)** — are treated as first-class and given the deepest scrutiny. I assume the SPEC's own mitigations are *claims to be falsified*, not facts.

---

## 0. Verdict up front

G3's two crux mechanisms (chain-continuous restore via `restore_boundary`, and the per-criticality circuit breaker) are **the right shape**, and unusually, the SPEC anticipated its own three headline attacks and wrote mitigations for them. But anticipating an attack in prose is not defeating it. **Three of the mitigations are under-specified at exactly the point where they'd actually fail**, and there are **two attacks the SPEC did not see** (A-6 reconciliation-as-injection, A-7 the drill-cadence false-confidence gap). None is fatal to the architecture; all are correctible. Prioritized list in §9.

---

## A-1 (CRITICAL) — Split-brain on the hash chain after a partial restore

**The attack.** The SPEC's split-brain defense is the **single-writer-per-source-chain fence** (R-G3-MR-3/4) plus "if a fork is detected anyway, reconcile preserving both tails" (R-G3-MR-5). I attack both halves.

**A-1a — The fence has a TOCTOU gap at failover.** R-G3-MR-6 says "fence the old primary *before* promoting the standby." But fencing a *dead* region is the easy case; the dangerous case is a **gray failure / network partition** where the old primary is *alive and still committing to its local quorum* but unreachable from the operator declaring the disaster. The operator promotes the standby (which has its own quorum in its region), and now **two live quorums are each advancing the same source-chain's `chain_seq`** — the textbook split-brain. The SPEC's fence is described as a step in a runbook, not as a **mechanically enforced quorum-membership change**. A runbook step a human performs under disaster stress is exactly where this fails.
- **Why the SPEC's quorum claim doesn't fully save it:** R-G3-MR-4 asserts "a minority partition cannot reach write-quorum and therefore cannot advance the chain." True *if* the two regions share one quorum membership. But in the **active-passive (P2) async** profile — the *default* recommended for most deployments — the standby is a **separate quorum**, not a minority of the primary's quorum. Async replication means the standby was *designed* to commit independently after promotion. So the "minority can't commit" guarantee **only holds in P3 (active-active sync)**, not in the P2 profile the SPEC recommends as default for non-regulated and even prices as the warm-standby norm. **The SPEC's strongest split-brain guarantee silently does not apply to its default topology.** This is the real defect.
- **Required fix:** promotion in P2 MUST be gated on a **STONITH-style fence token / epoch number** that is part of the chain itself: each promotion increments a monotonic **`chain_epoch`** baked into `chain_seq` (e.g. `(epoch, seq)`), and a writer can only commit if it holds the current epoch lease from a **single external arbiter** (a small, separately-deployed consensus quorum — even 3 nodes — that survives region loss and is the *sole* source of "who is primary"). A revived old primary discovers its epoch is stale and **refuses to commit / converts to buffer-and-reconcile**. Without an external arbiter that both regions consult, P2 failover is a coin-flip on split-brain. The SPEC's `restore_boundary` marker records *that* a failover happened but does nothing to *prevent* the old primary from continuing to write — it's a post-hoc label, not a fence.

**A-1b — Fork reconciliation is correct but its trigger is too late.** R-G3-MR-5 reconciles a *detected* fork by preserving both tails. Good — deleting evidence would be worse. But detection relies on "two divergent signed tails are ever detected." **Who runs that detection, how often, and against what?** If the two tails live in two regions that just healed, and the chain-verify job runs per-region, **each region's verify passes locally** (each tail is internally consistent) and the fork is invisible until someone diffs the two regions' heads. The SPEC has no **cross-region head-reconciliation check**. A fork could persist undetected for the entire window between failover and the next manual diff — during which auditors could be handed exports from *one* tail that silently omit the other tail's events.
- **Required fix:** a continuous **cross-region chain-head consistency check** (compare `(epoch, seq, head_hash)` across all replicas) that fires a Sev-1 the moment two heads share an ancestor checkpoint but diverge — making A-1b detection automatic, not "if someone notices."

## A-2 (HIGH) — The `restore_boundary` marker is a forgeable license to truncate

**The attack.** R-G3-RS-3/4 say a chain discontinuity *with* a valid signed `restore_boundary` marker is "a legitimate restore, not tamper," while a discontinuity *without* one is tamper. So the `restore_boundary` marker is **a signed permission slip to drop every event after a chosen `chain_seq`.** An insider who can produce a validly-signed marker (or who compromises the dual-control signing) can **truncate the chain at will and label it "we restored from backup"** — and the auditor's `/verify`, per the SPEC's own rule, will report it as *legitimate*. The very mechanism that prevents false tamper-alarms is a real tamper-*enabler*.
- **Why dual-control isn't enough:** R-G3-RS-3 requires "operator+platform dual key." But the *threat C2 §7.1 names* is a malicious admin — exactly someone with operator privileges. Dual-control raises the bar to two colluding operators (or one operator + a compromised platform key), which in a small SRE team is not a high bar.
- **What's missing:** a `restore_boundary` marker is only legitimate if there was an **actual disaster** and the dropped events were **genuinely unrecoverable**. The SPEC lets the marker *assert* a gap without *proving* one. Required: (1) the marker MUST reference the **WORM backup digest it restored from**, and the backup's own immutable existence + its last-checkpoint Merkle root must be **independently checkable** by the auditor — so a verifier confirms "the restored prefix exactly matches an immutable backup that was sealed *before* the claimed disaster," making a fabricated truncation detectable (the dropped events would have to also be absent from the immutable WORM copy, which the attacker cannot alter). (2) The dropped range MUST be **cross-checked against edge buffers and the cross-region replica** before the marker is accepted — if those *still hold* events the marker claims were lost, the marker is fraudulent and the discontinuity reverts to a **tamper finding**. (3) Marker issuance itself is an append-only, separately-witnessed event (e.g. counter-signed by the WORM store's own attestation, or a transparency-log entry). Without these, A-2 turns the anti-tamper primitive into the cleanest possible tamper tool.

## A-3 (CRITICAL) — RPO>0 means the audit log loses the auditor's evidence

**The attack — and it is the brief's named fatal one.** The SPEC's default profile (P1/P2) accepts **RPO ≤60s (async region loss)** and **RPO ≤15m (restore-from-WORM after the last checkpoint)** for D0. For a *compliance evidence product*, **any** lost decision event is a lost piece of the auditor's evidence — the one thing the platform exists to never lose. "We lost 60 seconds of your enforcement decisions" is, to a PCAOB/SOC2 auditor, indistinguishable from "your control had a 60-second hole we cannot account for." The SPEC's defense is "the loss is bounded, measured, and disclosed (`evidence_lost_in_failover`), never silent" (R-G3-RPO-2, R-G3-RS-6). **I attack the sufficiency of disclosure.**

- **A-3a — Disclosure does not restore the evidence; it documents its absence.** META G-6 already established that an auditor "does not accept 'we honestly told you we don't know' as evidence of a control operating effectively." A disclosed `evidence_lost_in_failover` gap is precisely an honest "we don't know what happened in those 60 seconds." So for the **regulated buyer (Stack C), RPO>0 on D0 is not acceptable at all**, disclosure or not — which means the SPEC's *default* (P1/P2) is **not a valid configuration for the buyer the product is actually sold to.** The SPEC half-admits this (R-G3-RPO-3 "SHOULD escalate to MUST for regulated"), but then leaves P1/P2 as the *default* and P3 as an *election*. **The default is wrong for the target market.** For the regulated profile, RPO=0 (P3 active-active sync, or at minimum synchronous cross-region commit for D0) must be the **mandated** default, with the latency/cost owned explicitly — not an opt-in the buyer might miss.

- **A-3b — Even P3's "RPO=0" has a tail the SPEC waves past.** R-G3-RPO-1 makes RPO=0 contingent on "committed = quorum across ≥2 failure domains." But the event is only D0 *after* it's committed; **before** commit it lives in the edge buffer (D0-pending). If the edge node and the region die *simultaneously* (correlated failure: e.g. a regional power event takes out both the spoke and the regional quorum), the buffered-but-uncommitted events are **gone**, and they were never replicated anywhere. So even P3 is RPO=0 only for *committed* events; the **commit-latency window is an irreducible RPO>0 for in-flight evidence**. The SPEC's R-G3-DC-2 calls the buffer "D0-pending" and demands durability, but a *local* disk buffer does not survive the node's destruction. **Required honesty:** the true RPO floor for D0 is `max(0, commit_latency)` and the buffer must itself be **replicated to ≥2 failure domains before the admission decision is even returned** if you want true zero-loss — which couples admission latency to cross-domain replication and may be unacceptable on the ≤2s hot path (B5-R6). This is a genuine, unresolved **latency-vs-durability tension on the hot path** the SPEC does not confront: you cannot have RPO=0 for D0 *and* a fast local-only buffer *and* survive correlated node+region loss. Pick two. The SPEC implies all three.

- **A-3c — Reconciliation from edge buffers (R-G3-RS-6) is the recovery story, but it widens A-2.** Recovering lost events from edge buffers after failover means **appending old events to the new authoritative tail out of their original position**. An attacker controlling a spoke could **inject fabricated "recovered" events** into the post-failover chain under the cover of reconciliation (they're expected to arrive late and out of order). See A-6.

## A-4 (CRITICAL) — The circuit breaker becomes a bypass

**The attack — the brief's second named one.** The breaker downgrades enforcement (deny→warn, or fail-open) when a shared dependency looks unhealthy. **An attacker who can make a dependency *look* unhealthy can therefore disable enforcement on demand**, then push a non-compliant/ malicious deploy through the warn-instead-of-deny window. The dependency probe (R-G3-CB-1) is the attack surface: make the signature verifier slow (a trivial DoS on the verifier, or just flood it), the breaker opens for the image-signing control, and **unsigned images now admit-with-warning fleet-wide.** The breaker is a remotely-triggerable enforcement kill switch.

- **The SPEC's defenses, evaluated:**
  - *Per-control granularity + C-CRITICAL safety controls stay hard-deny (R-G3-AV-2, §4.1):* **This is the actual defense, and it mostly works** — for C-CRITICAL safety controls (image-signing, priv-esc), the breaker does **not** downgrade to warn; it keeps hard-deny. So the headline attack (slip an unsigned image) is blocked *in C-CRITICAL scopes*. **But** the SPEC's default for C-STANDARD is warn-and-admit, and the attacker's target is rarely the most-locked-down namespace — it's the C-STANDARD prod namespace that's still sensitive but not classified C-CRITICAL. The defense's strength is **entirely dependent on correct criticality classification**, which is a human governance act the SPEC does not validate. **An under-classified namespace (C-STANDARD that should be C-CRITICAL) is a one-trigger bypass.** Required: a **default-deny-on-ambiguity** rule — any control bound to a Gemara control of regulated/safety category is treated as C-CRITICAL-equivalent for breaker purposes *regardless* of namespace classification, so misclassification fails safe.
  - *Loud, scoped, audited, time-bounded + §19 catch-up (R-G3-AV-3, R-G3-CB-6):* good — the attacker is **not invisible**, and §19 re-scan will *eventually* catch the slipped resource. **But there's a race:** the §19 catch-up runs *after* breaker close (R-G3-CB-6). A short-lived malicious workload (a crypto-miner, an exfiltration job) that **runs and exits during the degraded window** may be gone before catch-up evaluates it. Detective-after-the-fact does not stop a smash-and-grab. Required: for C-STANDARD-and-above, degraded admissions of **net-new workloads** (vs. updates to existing) SHOULD additionally trigger a **fast-path async re-evaluation within the degraded window** (not just post-recovery), so the catch-up latency for net-new admits is seconds, not "after the outage ends."

- **A-4b — Triggering the breaker open is itself the attack; can the attacker *hold it open*?** The probe failure threshold + sustained-recovery anti-flap (R-G3-CB-4) means an attacker who can sustain the dependency's degradation **holds the breaker open indefinitely**, extending the enforcement gap arbitrarily. The SPEC's anti-flap protects against false closes but **helps the attacker keep it open.** Required: an **absolute max-degraded-duration** per breaker, after which the disposition **escalates back toward fail-closed** (or pages for mandatory human decision) rather than sitting in warn forever — bounding the attacker's window regardless of how long they can DoS the dependency. "Warn indefinitely" is not a safe steady state for a security control.

- **A-4c — Who can trigger the manual override (R-G3-CB-8)?** Manual *open* requires only "an authorized operator + reason." Manual open of the breaker **is** the bypass, performed legitimately. The SPEC requires dual-control only for manual *force-close* of C-CRITICAL, not for manual *open*. An insider with operator role can manually open the breaker and walk a deploy through. Required: manual **open** of a breaker affecting C-CRITICAL/C-STANDARD prod scopes MUST also be dual-control and time-boxed, symmetric with force-close.

## A-5 (HIGH) — Restore re-opens closed bypasses (stale exceptions/approvals)

**The attack.** R-G3-CFG-2 includes exceptions/approvals in D1 backup. Consider: an exception for `payments-prod` is created, used, then **revoked/expired** during the day. That evening the cluster suffers a disaster and is restored from a **morning** backup — which contains the exception in its *active* state. The restore **silently re-opens a bypass that was deliberately closed.** Worse, an approval that was *denied* after the backup point comes back as *pending* or the underlying request re-fires. Restore-to-a-point-in-time on *security control state* is a **rollback of security decisions**, and the SPEC's D1 restore (R-G3-CFG-1) just re-applies the backup wholesale.
- **The SPEC's defense:** CHAOS-11 and a one-line "exceptions re-validated against expiry on restore" (FMEA-AP1). That covers *expiry* but **not revocation** (an exception explicitly revoked, not yet expired) and not *post-backup denials*. Required: D1 restore of security-control state (exceptions, approvals, role grants) MUST **reconcile against an append-only D0 record of grant/revoke/deny events** (these *are* C2 events) rather than trust the point-in-time snapshot — i.e. replay the security-decision log forward from the backup point to re-derive current grant state, and **never restore a security grant to a more-permissive state than the D0 log supports.** This makes D0 (the append-only, RPO-0 log) authoritative over D1 (the snapshot) for *security* state — which is the correct trust ordering and a nice reuse of the evidence log.

## A-6 (HIGH) — Reconciliation is an event-injection channel *(SPEC did not see this)*

**The attack.** Two SPEC mechanisms append events *out of band, after the fact, expected to be irregular*: edge-buffer reconciliation after failover (R-G3-RS-6) and fork reconciliation (R-G3-MR-5). Both **legitimately add events to an authoritative chain after a disruption, out of original order.** This is the perfect cover for **injecting fabricated history**: an attacker who controls a spoke (or who can write to the "recovered" buffer) submits forged `policy.decision` events during reconciliation — e.g. fabricating an "allow" decision to retroactively legitimize a deploy that was actually denied, or fabricating evidence that a control *did* fire when it didn't. Because reconciliation is *expected* to append old/odd events, these don't trip the normal tamper checks.
- **Why it's not covered:** the SPEC's idempotency rule (N-C2-105, R-G3-BUF-2) dedupes on `raw_event_digest` — it prevents *double-counting real events*, not *injecting new fake ones*. A fabricated event has a fresh, valid-looking digest.
- **Required fix:** reconciled/recovered events MUST carry **provenance proving they predate the disaster** — e.g. each edge buffer is itself a **signed, sealed mini-chain** (the spoke signs its buffer segments continuously), so a recovered event must be covered by a spoke-buffer signature whose `signed_at` is **before** the disaster timestamp. An event "recovered" during reconciliation that is *not* covered by a pre-disaster spoke signature is **rejected, not appended.** Without this, every failover is an opportunity to rewrite history under the banner of recovery — and it would pass `/verify` because it was appended through the legitimate reconciliation path.

## A-7 (MED) — Drill cadence manufactures false DR confidence *(SPEC did not see this)*

**The attack on the *process*.** R-G3-TEST-1 sets DR drills at **quarterly** (full) / monthly (chaos). The metric `g3_last_successful_drill_age_days` is presented as DR-readiness. But: (1) a drill that passed *3 months ago* against *last quarter's data volume and topology* tells you little about restoring *today's* 90-days-larger chain — **restore RTO grows with chain length** (G1 scale), and a restore that met ≤4h at POC volume may blow it at 100× without anyone noticing until the real disaster. (2) Drills run in **staging**, which the SPEC admits (R-G3-TEST-2) — but staging is smaller and cleaner than prod; the drill that matters (prod-scale restore) is the one explicitly *not* run. **A green drill badge is a confidence signal disproportionate to what was actually tested.**
- **Required fix:** (a) tie drill cadence to **data-volume growth, not just calendar** — re-drill when the chain grows by X% since the last drill, because RTO is volume-sensitive; (b) make **restore RTO a continuously *projected* metric** (extrapolate measured restore rate × current chain size) so you know your *current* RTO between drills, not just your last-drilled one; (c) at least one drill per year MUST be at **production scale** (G1), or the RTO target is unproven for the volume that matters.

## A-8 (MED) — Inconsistencies & under-specifications vs. sibling components

- **A-8a (vs. G4):** R-G3-RS-7 requires "historical public keys / key-validity restorable before the data." But G4 owns key custody, and if G4's key store is itself lost in the same disaster, **G3's restore-verify cannot run** (no keys to verify the chain against). The SPEC assumes G4 recovers first but doesn't make it a hard ordering *contract* with a fallback. If the key store and data store share a failure domain, both die together and the chain is unverifiable. **Required:** the key-validity *public* material must be backed up **with** each D0 backup (R-G3-BK-1 gestures at "key-id references travel with the backup" but references aren't the public keys) — the public keys themselves (not private) should be embedded in/alongside the WORM backup so verification is self-contained even if G4's live store is gone.
- **A-8b (vs. G2):** R-G3-MR-7 defers cost to G2 but the SPEC's *default* (P1) and *recommended-for-regulated* (P3) have **order-of-magnitude different costs** (full second/third copy of a 7-year-retained growing evidence store). If G2's cost model isn't ready, the buyer can't actually *choose* the profile, and the SPEC's "explicitly select" (R-G3-MR-1) becomes "default to the cheap, wrong-for-regulated P1." The seam is named but the **default is set before the cost trade is visible** — a sequencing trap.
- **A-8c (vs. C2 freeze):** G3 introduces a new `infrastructure_degraded` disposition + `degraded_session_id` + `chain.restore_boundary`/`fork_reconciliation` event types as "additive, N-C2-FWD-compatible." But the META review's M-1 shows C2's "additive-only" claim is already strained (the disposition/obligations split needs a real field change). Adding a `disposition_context` is *probably* additive, but **G3 is asserting C2-additivity it doesn't own** — this must be ratified by C2, not assumed. If C2 is being un-frozen to `v1.0-rc` anyway (META M-1), G3's additions should be folded into that rc, not bolted on later.

**Bottom line.** The architecture survives — `restore_boundary`, per-criticality degraded mode, and quorum-commit are the right primitives. But the three named attacks each have a real residual hole: **the split-brain fence doesn't cover the default topology (A-1a)**, **the breaker's safety rests on un-validated human classification (A-4)**, and **RPO>0 is genuinely unacceptable for the actual buyer and the SPEC leaves it as the default (A-3a)**. Plus two unseen channels (reconciliation-as-injection A-6, drill false-confidence A-7). Fix the top 3 before any regulated go-live; fix 4–6 before GA.
