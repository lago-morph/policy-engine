# G4 — Key Management & Cryptographic Lifecycle — ADVERSARIAL REVIEW

**Component ID:** G4 · **Reviewer role:** red-team / hostile cryptographic auditor. First contact with the SPEC; mandate is to break the tamper-evidence thesis, not to admire it.
**One-line verdict:** The SPEC closes the D4-OQ-1 gap competently and its rotation-vs-history model is sound *if implemented exactly as written*, but the whole edifice still reduces to **one offline trust-root key and one organizational SoD boundary that is a process promise, not a control** — and several of its strongest claims (offline verification, timeline-aware revocation) are only as good as a timestamp the platform itself produces. The attacks below are ranked by how completely they defeat the product's reason to exist.

---

## A. Prioritized attacks

### A-1 (CRITICAL) — Rotation breaks historical verification: the auditor can no longer verify a 2-year-old package
**Attack:** The naïve key-management design (the one the corpus had *before* G4) is "one signing key; rotate it." Under that design, when the key rotates, the trust store holds the *new* public key, and a 2-year-old signed evidence package — which an external auditor is independently re-verifying per HL-18 — fails to verify because the old public key is gone. The auditor's workpapers, which assert independent verifiability, are now false. **This is the single highest-impact failure** because it converts the differentiator (auditor-trustable, long-horizon evidence) into a liability: the platform *claimed* offline verifiability and then silently revoked it by rotating a key.

**Does the SPEC defend?** Yes — this is the SPEC's spine (§5). The defense is `key_id`-resolves-key (G4-R7) + an append-only Key Transparency Log where **public keys are never removed** (G4-R8) + overlap-based rotation (G4-R10) + a gating acceptance test (G4-R11). The verifier resolves the *historical* `key_id`, not "the current key."

**Residual hole (the real finding):** The defense is correct **only if the KTL the verifier consults is itself complete and trustworthy at verification time, possibly years later and offline.** Two ways it still breaks:
1. **Trust-bundle staleness offline.** G4-R21 embeds a *slice* of the KTL in the export, so an auditor with only the 2-year-old package can verify it. **But the SPEC makes G4-R21 a MUST for the embedded slice yet G4-R20 (the full bundle) distributable "from a published location… not solely from the audited platform's live API."** If an implementer ships the slice but the slice omits the *revocation status* that materialized *after* the export was made, the auditor verifies a signature whose key was later revoked and never learns it. **Fix:** the offline verifier MUST treat "I cannot obtain a revocation feed at least as fresh as a stated horizon" as a *warning state*, not a clean pass — the SPEC currently lets an embedded-slice verification return clean without bounding revocation freshness. Add an explicit `revocation_freshness` field to the verifier output (G4-R23) and to the embedded slice.
2. **KTL loss = total verification loss.** The KTL is the index from `key_id` → public key. If the KTL is lost (it's an append-only store like the evidence — and the META-ADVERSARIAL pass flagged that **the evidence store itself has no DR/RPO owner, Risk #4**), then *no* historical signature can be resolved to a key, and **all** evidence becomes unverifiable — a strictly worse outcome than losing the evidence (you lose the ability to trust what remains). **The SPEC does not give the KTL its own backup/DR contract.** It should: the KTL must be at least as durable as the evidence it protects, and embedding the per-package slice (G4-R21) is the partial mitigation but only for packages already exported. **This is the most important gap in the SPEC** and points squarely at the unowned-DR risk; flag to G3 (availability/DR) explicitly.

**Verdict:** Architecture is right; **two concrete holes** (revocation-freshness on offline verify; KTL durability) must be closed or A-1 reopens.

### A-2 (CRITICAL) — Single root-of-trust compromise = forge any evidence
**Attack:** The SPEC consolidates trust into an offline trust-root (§7.1) that attests operational keys and signs the KTL head. **Whoever holds that root can mint a fraudulent operational key, attest it into a fraudulent KTL, sign a fraudulent checkpoint over fabricated events, and the whole chain verifies.** One key compromise forges arbitrary evidence with full cryptographic validity. For a tamper-evidence product, a single forge-anything key is the worst possible single point of failure.

**Does the SPEC defend?** Partially. It minimizes the root's surface (offline/air-gapped, rare use, dual-control ceremony, G4-R18/R26) and acknowledges §9.5 as "accepted residual risk." The out-of-band trust-root distribution (G4-R22) is the real defense: an auditor pins the root key from *multiple independent channels*, so a forged KTL signed by a *different* (stolen-but-not-the-pinned) root is caught.

**Residual hole:** G4-R22's multi-channel distribution is asserted but not *operationalized*. "Published through documentation, `.well-known`, the signed export, ideally a third-party mirror" — three of those four channels are **controlled by the same operator** who could be the very party forging evidence. Only the "third-party/notary mirror" is genuinely independent, and it's the one marked "ideally" (i.e. optional). **An operator who can edit the docs, the `.well-known` endpoint, and the export can present a self-consistent forged root to a first-time auditor.** The defense only works against an auditor who pinned the *genuine* root *before* the compromise. **Fix:** make at least one *operator-independent* trust-root publication channel a MUST (e.g. a public transparency log / notary / the customer's own escrow), not "ideally." Until then, A-2's defense is theatre against an insider who controls the platform's own publication surfaces — which is exactly the threat model the product exists to counter (insider deletion/edit, C2 §7.1).

**Deeper point:** the SPEC's own §6.4 external timestamp anchor is the *better* answer to both A-2 and A-1 and it is **GA-deferred (SHOULD→MUST@GA)**. Anchoring to an *external* clock/ledger is what removes the "trust the platform's own signature/timestamp" circularity. Deferring it to GA means **the POC's tamper-evidence story is self-referential**: the platform signs, the platform timestamps, the platform publishes the root. A sophisticated insider defeats all three. The honest POC framing is: *"POC tamper-evidence detects outsider/casual tampering and honest-operator mistakes; it does NOT yet defend against a malicious operator who controls the publication surfaces — that requires the GA external anchor."* The SPEC should say this plainly rather than imply the POC already delivers auditor-grade independence.

### A-3 (CRITICAL) — In-cluster keys = anyone with cluster-admin forges the audit chain
**Attack:** If the audit-checkpoint signing key (K1) is a Kubernetes Secret or in-cluster file, then **any `cluster-admin` — the platform's own operators, a compromised operator credential, a malicious insider — can read the key, edit a past event, recompute the chain, and re-sign every checkpoint.** The hash chain + Merkle checkpoints become worthless because the attacker holds the signing key. This **directly defeats the entire tamper-evidence thesis** (C2 §7.4's threat model assumes the attacker does *not* have the key). It is the most common way real-world "tamper-evident" logs are actually defeated.

**Does the SPEC defend?** Yes, and this is the SPEC's best decision: G4-R4 **forbids** K1/K2 from living in any cluster-admin-readable location and mandates KMS/HSM non-exportable custody; G4-R27 separates KMS IAM ownership from cluster operation; the M-G4-SOD test (PLAN §6.3) literally attempts the exfiltration as a cluster-admin and requires it to fail. The ALT's whole case for KMS-over-in-cluster turns on exactly this.

**Residual hole:** KMS custody stops key *exfiltration* but not *capability hijack* — a cluster-admin who controls the signer **workload identity** can still *request* signatures (the SPEC concedes this in §9.3 / G4-R6). And **G4-R27's separation ("security-ops owns KMS IAM, not the cluster operator") is an organizational boundary, not a technical control.** In a POC — and in many real customers — the same small team runs the cluster *and* the KMS. If the person who can `kubectl exec` into the signer pod also has KMS-invoke rights (the default, easiest setup), A-3 is only *partially* mitigated: they can't steal the key, but they can drive it to sign whatever they fabricate, bounded only by the rate/context limits in G4-R6 — which are **SHOULD→MUST@GA**, i.e. not guaranteed at POC. **So at POC, a cluster-admin with signer-invoke rights can still forge the chain**, just at the signer's allowed cadence. **Fix:** G4-R6 (rate/context limits) and the binding of signer-invoke to a workload identity *distinct from any human cluster-admin* must be **MUST at POC**, not GA — otherwise the headline custody win is undercut by the invocation path. The SPEC half-sees this (§9.3) but leaves the closing control GA-deferred.

### A-4 (HIGH) — HSM cost vs POC budget
**Attack:** Real HSMs (CloudHSM dedicated instances, on-prem appliances) cost thousands/month and add operational complexity (clustering, backup, quorum). A POC on a startup budget (F3 §22 is explicitly a small functional POC) cannot justify dedicated-HSM spend, so the team will be tempted to "just use a Secret for now" — which lands straight back in A-3 and silently defeats the thesis. The cost pressure is the *mechanism* by which the most important control gets skipped.

**Does the SPEC defend?** Yes, well: G4-OQ-2 decides **cloud KMS (HSM-backed key-protection level), not a dedicated HSM, at POC** — non-exportable keys + IAM + audit at near-zero marginal cost (a managed KMS key is cents/month + per-operation pennies), with the dedicated-HSM upgrade as a GA option behind an *identical interface*. This neutralizes the budget excuse for skipping custody: KMS gives the A-3 defense cheaply.

**Residual hole:** The SPEC asserts "interface is identical, so GA hardening is a config change" — **true for the signing API, not necessarily for the trust ceremony.** Moving from a cloud-KMS trust root to an air-gapped HSM ceremony (G4-OQ-5) at GA is *not* a config change; it's a key-generation ceremony + re-attestation + re-publication of the root through all channels — i.e. effectively an A-2-adjacent trust-root rotation, with all the multi-channel-republication fragility A-2 flagged. The SPEC underplays this as "GA hardening." **The cost the SPEC didn't model:** the *operational* cost of running KMS correctly at production evidence volume — every checkpoint and every export is a KMS sign operation; at the production scale the META-ADVERSARIAL pass flagged as unmodeled (Risk #2, 10–100M events/day), per-operation KMS calls + their latency + their cost are a real line item that G4 inherits and does not quantify. Flag to G2 (cost/retention economics): **KMS sign-operation cost at production checkpoint/export volume is unmodeled.**

### A-5 (HIGH) — Timeline-aware revocation rests on a timestamp the attacker may control
**Attack:** The SPEC's clever recovery model (§6, §9.2) hinges on distinguishing signatures made *before* a key leak (valid) from *after* (invalid), using `signed_at`. But the SPEC itself admits `signed_at` is attacker-controllable if the attacker holds the signing capability (G4-R15). It then falls back to "the audit chain's own ordering + the previous checkpoint's independently-trusted `signed_at`." **This is circular at the boundary:** if the attacker compromised the key at time T and the *previous* checkpoint was also signed by the same (now-compromised) key, then the "independently-trusted previous timestamp" is *not* independently trusted — it's signed by the same bad key. The chain-ordering argument only works if there's a checkpoint signed by a *different, uncompromised* key (or an external anchor) before the leak. Without external anchoring, **a long-undetected key compromise lets the attacker backdate forgeries behind the leak boundary**, and the timeline model can't catch it.

**Does the SPEC defend?** It points to G4-R17 external timestamp anchoring as the real fix — **but that's GA-deferred (§6.4, §9.4).** At POC, the timeline model is sound only against compromises detected quickly (before the next differently-anchored checkpoint), and weak against a patient insider. **Fix:** the SPEC should state the limit honestly: *"POC timeline-aware revocation is reliable only for the window between external-anchor-equivalents; for the POC that means it degrades to `best_effort` trust for any pre-detection signatures, consistent with the honesty tenet."* The SPEC's §9.2 step 3 *does* fall back to `best_effort` — good — but it presents the chain-ordering argument as stronger than it is when the whole chain is single-keyed. Make the dependency on §6.4 explicit as a POC *limitation*, not just a GA *enhancement*.

### A-6 (MEDIUM) — Two trust domains, but the verifier and operators may collapse them
**Attack:** §8 mandates separate trust domains (audit root K1 ≠ bundle K3 ≠ mesh K6), so compromising the supply-chain signer can't forge audit evidence. Good. **But the offline verifier (G4-R24) verifies *both* domains, and the operator who builds/distributes the verifier could ship a verifier that lies** (reports "valid" for a forged signature). The trust then shifts to the verifier binary's own provenance — which is signed by K4 (platform image signing, a *third* domain). Circular again: to trust the verifier you must trust K4; to trust K4 you verify with... the verifier. **Fix:** the verifier must be independently buildable from published source (reproducible build) and its release signature checkable by a *generic* cosign/Sigstore client the auditor already trusts — not only by the platform's own tooling. The SPEC says "standalone, no platform dependency" (G4-R23) but doesn't address verifier *provenance* / the bootstrap-trust-in-the-verifier problem.

---

### A-7 (MEDIUM) — Salt-rotation generational boundary is a quiet migration landmine
**Attack:** G4-R12 forbids rotating the redaction salt (K8) within a retention window because it would break `content_hash`. But salts *do* need rotation eventually (a leaked salt makes redaction-preserving hashes brute-forceable for low-entropy claims like `email`). The "generational boundary + re-hash forward" is named but **not specified** — re-hashing a sealed, hash-chained, checkpoint-signed segment is exactly the C2 "re-chain across a schema change" migration the META-ADVERSARIAL pass flagged as unplanned (Risk #8). G4 inherits that unplanned migration for salts and waves at it. **Fix:** either commit to "salts never rotate, accept the brute-force risk and raise per-claim entropy," or own the generational re-hash migration jointly with C2/G7 — don't leave it as a one-line gesture.

---

### A-8 (LOW) — `key_id` = hash of public key leaks nothing but couples identity to the key
**Observation:** G4-R3 derives `key_id` from the public key. Clean (collision ⇒ same key, no reuse). Minor consequence: you cannot pre-allocate a `key_id` before generating the key, and you cannot "re-issue the same `key_id` with a new key" (which is correct — you shouldn't — but worth noting it forecloses some HSM key-versioning models where the cloud KMS rotates *under* a stable key resource name). Ensure the KMS integration records the *material* version's public key as the `key_id` source, not the KMS resource name, or KMS-side auto-rotation will silently change the signing key under a stable `key_id` and break G4-R7 resolution. **This is a real implementation trap**, low severity but easy to get wrong.

## C. Bottom line

The SPEC **does** resolve D4-OQ-1 and **does** build the right spine (KMS custody + append-only KTL + offline verifier + timeline-aware revocation) — it genuinely answers the META-ADVERSARIAL "the key leaked, what about 3 years of checkpoints?" question, which nothing else in the corpus did. The architecture survives.

**But its POC posture oversells independence.** Three of the four CRITICAL/HIGH defects (D-G4-3, D-G4-4, D-G4-5) share one root cause: **the controls that make the scheme defend against a *malicious operator* — invocation-identity separation, operator-independent root publication, and external timestamp anchoring — are all GA-deferred, while the SPEC's framing implies POC-grade auditor independence.** A POC that protects against outsiders and honest mistakes but not against the insider who runs the platform is still valuable and still honest — *if it says so*. The most important single change is to either pull a minimal external anchor + operator-independent root channel into the POC, or to label the POC's tamper-evidence as "outsider/mistake-grade, not malicious-operator-grade" — consistent with the platform's own honesty-over-coverage tenet. And the most important *latent* defect is D-G4-1: the KTL is now a second system-of-record whose loss is catastrophic, and like the evidence store before it, **it currently has no DR owner.**
